2. What we did, in four steps

1. Define pressureMoments when one ball can swing the match (win-probability model).
2. Set a fair baselineWhat an average player would do in the same situation.
3. Score playersActual minus expected, plus a player’s own usual level and consistency.
4. Stress-testRe-rank 400 times with different matches, weights and pressure definitions.

Data: 815 IPL and T20I matches (2022 to 2026-11), 187,042 balls faced. Super overs and rain-affected matches are left out.

3. What counts as pressure

A ball is “pressure” when the match result can swing a lot on it. We built a win-probability model (chase accuracy AUC 0.886, first innings 0.783) and measured how much each ball can move it.

Batting pressure (about 17% of balls)

  • The top 10% of balls by how much the result can swing, or
  • Last pair batting with 2+ overs left; a collapse (4 wickets down inside 12 overs, or 3 down inside 6) while behind; two quick early wickets; a very hard chase (target 15%+ above par) early on.

Bowling pressure (about 21% of balls)

  • Death overs with the match still open (30–70% win chance); being hit for 26+ in 12 balls with the game live; a tight chase (43–57% win chance); or the top 10% of high-swing balls.

A second, broader definition (33% of balls) is also used. The final list averages both, so it does not depend on one choice.

4. How players are scored

Every ball has an expected result for an average player in that spot, using batting position, partner, bowler (or batter) quality, venue, opposition strength, target and match state. A player’s performance is actual minus expected, so openers, finishers and death bowlers are each judged against their own role.

Small samples are pulled towards the average

A player with few pressure innings can look great or terrible by luck. Every metric is “shrunk” towards the average by exactly as much as its noise requires. A metric with no repeatable signal is shrunk to zero automatically.

Important finding. Only some metrics carry real, repeatable signal. In batting about 98% of the final score comes from one metric (runs above expected under pressure); delta, relief, reliability and tail barely move the result. In bowling, runs saved explains about 86%, share of positive spells 10% and pressure delta 4%. The weights above are what we chose; the chart shows what the data lets them do.

5. Fair comparisons

  • Same baseline for everyone: a player is only compared with an average player in the same spot, so no role is automatically favoured.
  • Minimum sample: batters need 60+ pressure balls and 8+ pressure innings; bowlers 80+ balls and 8+ spells. Players with fewer than 10 pressure innings carry a “few pressure innings” flag in the dashboard.
  • Lucky streaks are discounted: balls inside one innings are not independent, so the model counts innings, not just balls.
  • IPL and T20I together: both are used, with allowance for league, opposition strength (IPL table, ICC ratings) and venue. The dashboard can re-score any year window and either league separately.
  • Match importance and recency: knock-out and must-win games count more; recent matches count a little more (3-year half-life). Both are tested by removing them.
  • Everyone is ranked: no “active in 2026” filter in the submitted list, because the brief does not ask for one.

6. How the ranking is built

  • Clutch Meter = 50 + 10 × the player’s average standardised score over 400 stress runs. Each run changes three things: which matches are included (resampling), how much each metric weighs (±35%) and which pressure definition is used (strict or broad).
  • Players are ranked strictly by Clutch Meter. Averaging over the runs means the list does not depend on any single modelling choice of ours.
  • “In top 10 in” and the rank range are shown beside every rank as confidence: a high chance and a short bar mean a firm rank.

Bars show where each player lands across the 400 runs (5th to 95th percentile); dots are medians. Long bars mean an uncertain rank.

Plain-language limits

  • Batting signal is weak. With only 8–40 pressure innings per player, much of the ordering beyond the top few is luck. That is why we report “chance of being in the top 10” beside every rank.
  • The pressure definition matters. Using only strict or only broad pressure changes the batting top 10 by 2–3 names, bowling by 0–1.
  • The result leans on IPL data. Internationals-only samples are small, so the international top 10 is quite different. Treat league views as supporting detail.
  • Death-over bowlers score a little higher on average (7 of the top 10 bowl many death overs), because their pressure overs are by construction the highest-stakes. Adjusting for this keeps 9 of the 10.
  • Missing runs are estimated. The data does not record bye and leg-bye runs, so about 385 match totals were reconciled with the result text and the gap was allocated to extras balls.
  • Team strength looks slightly ahead (season-end tables and year-end ratings), not strictly “as known before the match”.
